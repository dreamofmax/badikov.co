Why I still shoot documentary work on film

Max Badikov — documentary photographer

Shooting documentary work on film forces a kind of discipline that digital doesn't ask for. A roll holds a finite number of frames, so each one has to be considered before the shutter goes. That constraint changes how a photographer moves through a scene — less spraying frames hoping one lands, more waiting for the moment the picture is actually there.

The Nikon F3 and Leica are both built for that kind of patience: mechanical, quiet, unobtrusive in a room where people are trying to forget a camera is present at all. Neither camera does anything to help a photographer who hasn't already decided what they're looking for — no autofocus to hide behind, no burst mode to paper over a missed moment.

Black-and-white processing adds a second layer of restraint. Removing colour strips a scene down to shape, gesture, and light, which tends to make a documentary frame read as more honest rather than less — there's nowhere for a striking colour to do the work a composition should be doing instead.

None of this is a rejection of digital tools generally — picture editing work happens entirely in that world. But for the documentary practice itself, the limits of film are the point, not an inconvenience to work around.
