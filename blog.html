Documentary Photography Notes

Max Badikov — documentary & editorial photographer, picture editor

This is a working notebook on documentary photography — how a frame gets made, how it gets chosen, and what changes when a photograph moves from a negative to a printed page. It sits alongside a portfolio built on black-and-white film, shot mostly on a Nikon F3 and a Leica, and a picture-editing practice shaped by several years inside a newsroom.

Why film, still

Shooting documentary work on film forces a kind of discipline that digital doesn't ask for. A roll holds a finite number of frames, so each one has to be considered before the shutter goes. That constraint changes how a photographer moves through a scene — less spraying frames hoping one lands, more waiting for the moment the picture is actually there. The Nikon F3 and Leica are both built for that kind of patience: mechanical, quiet, unobtrusive in a room where people are trying to forget a camera is present at all.

What picture editing teaches a photographer

Years spent on the editing side — sequencing, captioning, and selecting other photographers' work for publication — change how you shoot your own. An editor learns to see a set of frames as a story rather than a collection of good shots, and that habit carries back into the field. The best frame in a sequence is rarely the most technically perfect one; it's the one that earns its place next to the others.

Found and anonymous photographs

Alongside new documentary work, there's an ongoing interest in anonymous found family photographs — pictures stripped of their original context, owners, and captions, and what they still manage to communicate once that context is gone. Collecting and studying this kind of vernacular photography is its own way of thinking about what makes a documentary image legible decades after it was made.
